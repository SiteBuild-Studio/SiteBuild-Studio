Do You Actually Need a Website for Your Small Business?

A straight answer, backed by the numbers, for landscapers, salons, cafés, and everyone in between.

6 min read  ·  SiteBuild Studio

If your business has survived on word of mouth, a Facebook page, and a good reputation so far, it's a completely fair question: do you actually need a website, or is that just something agencies say to sell websites?

The honest answer is that it depends on where your customers are looking for you — and increasingly, research shows that's happening somewhere you might not be.

Where people actually look for you first

Local search behaviour has shifted more than most business owners realise. Industry research from BrightLocal and SOCi's Consumer Behavior Index consistently finds that around 80% of consumers search for local businesses online at least once a week, and roughly a third do so every single day. That's not people browsing — that's people actively trying to find a business like yours, right now, before they've decided who to call.

Research on "near me" searches specifically found that 76% of people who search this way visit a business within 24 hours. By the time someone searches, they're usually close to deciding — the question is just whether they can find you.

This matters just as much for a pool maintenance company or a hair salon as it does for a shop. Service businesses are searched for locally just as often — sometimes more, because people are actively comparing before they commit to a booking or a quote.

What happens when you're not there

Estimates vary by source, but most recent research converges on a similar figure: roughly a quarter to a third of small businesses still don't have a website. That number has been shrinking steadily for years, which means the businesses without one are increasingly the exception, not the norm.

The businesses in that group aren't necessarily struggling. Many are established, busy, and doing fine on referrals. But "doing fine" and "not losing customers you never see" are different things. If someone searches for what you do and only your competitors show up with a website, a set of hours, and a way to contact them, you were never in the running — not because you lost the sale, but because you were never visible enough to compete for it.

A website does more than just "look professional"

It's tempting to think of a website mainly as a trust signal — a way to look legitimate. That's part of it, but the more concrete value shows up in how people actually behave once they find you.

  • Consumers are significantly more likely to visit or consider a business when they can find complete, consistent information about it online — not just a name and a phone number, but hours, services, and a way to reach you.
  • Inconsistent or missing information cuts the other way just as strongly: a meaningful share of consumers say they'll actively avoid a business if the information they find online doesn't match up, or is missing entirely.
  • A website is the one piece of your online presence you fully control — no algorithm deciding who sees it, no platform changing the rules on you.

"Isn't a Facebook or Instagram page enough?"

For some businesses, social media is genuinely where their customers are — and it's worth using well. But it works best alongside a website, not instead of one, for a few practical reasons:

  • You don't own the platform. A layout change, an algorithm update, or an account issue is completely out of your hands — and it happens to real businesses more often than people expect.
  • It doesn't show up the same way in search. When someone searches Google for your business name, or "[service] near me," a real website with basic local SEO gives you a far better chance of actually appearing — a social profile alone often doesn't compete in the same results.
  • It's harder to act on. Booking a viewing, requesting a quote, or checking your prices is slower and clumsier through a social inbox than through a page built for exactly that.

The businesses that tend to do best usually aren't choosing one over the other — they're running a simple website as the "home base" that everything else, including social media, points back to.

What a website actually needs to do

None of this means a small business needs a large or expensive website. It means the basics need to be done properly:

  • Works cleanly on a phone, since most local searches happen on mobile.
  • Makes it obvious what you do and how to contact you, in the first few seconds.
  • Has the basic technical groundwork in place — fast loading, clear structure, and simple local SEO — so search engines can actually find and understand it.
  • Gets kept up to date, rather than published once and forgotten.

That's a genuinely achievable bar for a small business, without needing a large budget or an in-house developer — which is really the whole point.

The short version

Most of your customers are already searching for businesses like yours online before they ever pick up the phone. A website doesn't guarantee they'll choose you — but not having one all but guarantees a share of them never find you in the first place. For most small businesses today, that's less a marketing decision and more a basic cost of staying visible.

Figures referenced above are drawn from published research by BrightLocal, SOCi's Consumer Behavior Index, Zippia, and Google, current as of 2025–2026. Exact figures vary by source and region — treat them as directional trends rather than guarantees for any individual business.
